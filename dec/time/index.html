1 Annual day aggregate (ada)

The red line indicates a “day of year” (doy): , which identifies a day in a year like a Gregorian calendar month and “day of month” (dom). The red dot denotes a “time of day” (tod): , which specifies a point in a day like an “hour minute second” (hms) triplet. Together, a doy and tod can form an “annual day aggregate” (ada): + ÷ 1000 = .

\[ \begin{split} \text{doy} &= \lfloor\text{ada}\rfloor \\ &= \text{ada} - \text{tod} \end{split} \tag{1}\]

As their names suggest, doy and ada are measured in days. The measurement unit of a tod can be a day or a submultiple of a day with a metric prefix. By changing how a decimal tod is measured, we can shift its decimal separator or turn it into an integer. The tod along the y-axis of the area chart are integers because they have three digits and are measured in millidays.

2 Epochal day aggregate (eda)

To obtain a tod from a ada, we can keep the remainder after dividing by one to isolate the decimal part of the quotient: mod 1 = 0.. We can use this same approach to separate a tod from an “epochal day aggregate” (eda): mod 1 = 0.. The current eda tells us how many days have passed since the Dec epoch: Year 0 Day 0 Tod 0.

\[\begin{split} \text{tod} &= \text{ada} \bmod 1 \\ &= \text{eda} \bmod 1 \end{split} \tag{2}\]

UNIX time equation

Similarly, UNIX time tallies the seconds since the UNIX epoch, which is exactly 719468 days after the Dec epoch. To get the tod in Zone 0, the Dec time zone that is in between the two leftmost vertical lines on the map, we can divide UNIX time by the number of seconds in a day and then keep the remainder after dividing the resulting days by 1 as in Equation 3.

\[\text{tod} = \text{unix} \div 86400 \bmod 1 \tag{3}\]

Julian date equation

Julian dates track the days since the beginning of the Julian period and thus are akin to eda. We can produce a Zone 5 tod from a Julian date simply by keeping the remainder after dividing by one. If we want a Zone 0 tod instead, we should add 5 decidays to the Julian date, divide the sum by one, and then keep the remainder as in Equation 4 below.

\[\text{tod} = (\text{julian} + 0.5) \bmod 1 \tag{4}\]

4 Universal time offset (uto)

The Global Positioning System, BeiDou, and Galileo global navigation satellite systems along with most — if not all — programming languages do not account for leap seconds, which appears to be for the best given that leap seconds can cause problems and will be abolished no later than 2027+081 according to the International Bureau of Weights and Measures.

On or before the deadline to abolish leap seconds, the tolerance for disagreement between UTC and Universal Time (UT) will increase from 1 Dec second (s) to 3750 s. Since 1972+122, only 27 leap seconds have been inserted, which is 0.75% of 3750 s, and at this rate it will take over five millennia for the disparity between UT and UTC to reach 3750 s.

Instead of leap seconds, Dec matches UT using a “universal time offset” (uto). 6 Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)

Rather than use a terminator as in Table 3, Dec expresses one hour in dd as a single b24 digit: 0.41♄ = β. If we add β dd to the Zone 0 tod, we get the tod associated with the UTC+1 offset: 0. + β ÷ 10 = 0.. Currently, the Zone 0 Dec time is 0.−0 and the UTC+1 Dec time is 0.−β. Dec times consist of a tod, a minus sign (−), and an offset.

There are 12 UTC offsets that become a terminating decimals after being converted from hours to days and therefore could be displayed in either b10 or b24. Although it has only ten time zones, Dec can express any UTC offset using one or two non-negative b24 digits. There are 27 UTC offsets that require only one b24 digit and 11 that need two b24 digits.

Out of the 38 UTC offsets, 14 are negative (<UTC) and 24 are non-negative (≥UTC). The UTC time zone with the most negative offset is completely uninhabited. Dec forbids the association of dates with negative offsets. Therefore, Dec will add ten decidays to any negative offset and add one day to the date associated with that offset. Negative offset handling has no effect on the tod or the conversion of an hms triplet to a tod and is typically only necessary after a “year month day” (ymd) triplet is converted into a Dec date.

7 Day of week (dow)

Adding one day to the doy has no effect on the tod, but it increments the dom and “day of week” (dow). Dec meets the expectations of Gregorian calendar users who live in UTC time zones with negative offsets by essentially deriving the previous dom or dow from the current doy if a location in the Americas is provided without an offset or with a negative offset.

8 Time zone offset (tzo)

In Dec, “time zone offsets” (tzo) are derived solely from longitude. Dec measures longitude in wěi (w), arcbeats (a), or submultiples thereof. If we travel East or West until we reach our starting point, we will cover 1 w or 100000 (105) a. A tzo is essentially a deciwěi (dw) longitude that became a single-digit b10 integer via truncation or flooring: ⌊ ÷ 100⌋ = .

The Equator is approximately (~) 1 taur (c) or ~105 kilozem (kz) long. Near the Equator, a longitude that has a precision of 0.1 microwěi (µw) or 10 milliarcbeats (ma) is accurate to within ~0.1 microtaur (µc), ~10 “zone equatorial meters” (zem), or 4 meters, which is about the width of a U-shaped living room layout or the length of a typical subcompact car.

For precise geopositioning, we may want to express geographic coordinates in ma, but we are unlikely to need s or cmd when displaying the solar time or estimates of what the tod will be when the Sun rises, reaches its highest point of the day, or sets. By default, Dec uses md precision to display a solar time and the “solar time offset” (sto) used to produce it.

In Dec, any offset can be called a sto or a uto, because there will always be a longitude at which the sum of the given offset and the Zone 0 tod will yield solar time or UT. In contrast, Dec has only ten tzo, one for each of its ten time zones. Dec translates UTC to Zone 0 and UTC−12 or UTC+12 to Zone 5, but does not categorize any other UTC offset as a tzo.

Dec permits any non-negative offset that is less than one day and can be written with a finite number of b9, b10, and b24 digits. Dec times represent truncated math expressions that simplify to the Zone 0 tod: 0.−125÷1000 = 0.−δ÷10 = 0.. The UTC+3 tod is the sum of the Zone 0 tod and either δ dd or 125 md. Therefore, the current UTC+3 Dec time can be either 0.−δ or 0.−125.

9 Solar time offset (sto)

China is a good example of the difference between a sto and a tzo. The territory of China spans three Dec time zones, Zones 2, 3, and 4, but the government of China has decided that everyone in China should use UTC+08:00, which is Earth (♁) in b9 or iota (ι) in b24. Dec does not enforce the use of any offset, but it would suggest that users in China consider a tzo of 3 dd, which is not far off from 8/24, 1/3, ♁, or ι. Zone 3 may actually be closer to the sto of most people in China, because the center of Zone 3, 350 mw, is close to the geographic center of China, ~337 mw, whereas the central longitude of UTC+08:00, ~383 mw, is much further East.

To get a local solar time, we add a sto to a Zone 0 tod. If the sto accounts only for longitude, the result is an mst. If it also includes the eot, the result is an ast. Whereas a Dec time zone spans a dw, a sto represents a single longitude. In Equation 16 below, the sto of a longitude in w is (0.95 + w) mod 1.

10 Equation of time (eot)

The two types of solar time are “mean solar time” (mst) and “apparent solar time” (ast). To calculate an mst, we keep only the decimal part of the sum of 0.95, the Zone 0 tod measured in days, and a longitude measured in w. If we want ast instead of mst, the sum needs to include the result of plugging the “time of year” (toy) into the “equation of time” (eot).

\[\text{syl} = \text{leap} + 365 \tag{14}\]

\[\text{toy} = \text{ada} \div \text{syl} \tag{15}\]

\[\text{mst} = (0.95 + \text{tod} + \text{w}) \bmod 1 \tag{16}\]

\[\text{ast} = (0.95 + \text{tod} + \text{w} + \text{eot}) \bmod 1 \tag{17}\]

To obtain a toy, we divide an ada by a “solar year length” (syl), which is the number of days in the year. Dec passes the toy directly from Equation 15 to the eot function in Equation 18 without first multiplying it by 2𝜋 or 𝜏 to convert it from turns (t) to radians (rad) because the sin and cos trigonometric functions used by Dec are designed for t instead of rad.

\[\begin{split} \text{eot} & = \beta_0 \\ & + \beta_1 \ast \text{cos(toy)} \\ & + \beta_2 \ast \text{sin(toy)} \\ & + \beta_3 \ast \text{cos(2} \ast \text{toy)} \\ & + \beta_4 \ast \text{sin(2} \ast \text{toy)} \end{split} \tag{18}\]

12 Light arc whole (law)

The path in the sky that the Sun appears to follow from a sunrise to a sunset is a “light arc whole” (law). Solar noon, the midpoint of a law, is halfway between sunrise and sunset. A law can be evenly split into two “light arc halves” (lah). Whereas tod are time points, law and lah are durations. The difference between a solar noon tod and a lah is a sunrise tod.

\[\text{law} = \text{lah} \ast 2 \tag{24}\]

\[\begin{split} \text{sunrise} & = \text{solarmidnight} + \text{nah} \\ & = \text{solarnoon} - \text{lah} \\ & = \text{sunset} - \text{law} \end{split} \tag{25}\]

\[\begin{split} \text{sunset} & = \text{solarmidnight} + \text{nah} + \text{law} \\ & = \text{sunrise} + \text{law} \\ & = \text{solarnoon} + \text{lah} \\ \end{split} \tag{26}\]

Night arc whole (naw)

The Sun continues its path after it disappears below the horizon, moving from sunset to sunrise along a “night arc whole” (naw). Like conjugate angles, a law and a naw form a full circle that represents one day. Likewise, a lah and a “night arc half” (nah) are like two supplementary angles that form a semicircle from solar noon to the naw midpoint: solar midnight.

\[\text{naw} = \text{nah} \ast 2 \tag{27}\]

\[\begin{split} \text{day} & = \text{law} + \text{naw} \\ & = (\text{lah} + \text{nah}) \ast 2 \end{split} \tag{28}\]

\[\begin{split} \text{solarmidnight} &= \text{sunrise} - \text{nah} \\ &= \text{solarnoon} - \text{lah} - \text{nah} \\ &= \text{sunset} - \text{law} - \text{nah} \end{split} \tag{29}\]

The range input below controls the yellow law and the blue naw in the diagram beneath it. At the Equator, the sun always rises about a quarter turn from North and sets around three quarter turns from North, resulting in a law of roughly half a day: 75% − 25% = 50%. If the law is zero, a polar night occurs. A day without a naw is called a polar day.

The numbers in the diagram above are “solar azimuth angle” (saa) milliturns (mt). At solar noon, the saa is 0 mt in the Southern Hemisphere and 500 mt in the Northern Hemisphere. The radio📻input above controls whether the midpoint of the law is 0 or 500 mt. To measure the saa during the daytime, point a compass🧭at the spot on the horizon below the Sun.

During an equinox at the Equator, the Sun goes from East to West directly overhead, the saa flips like a switch from 0.25 t to 0.75 t at solar noon, the “solar elevation angle” (sea) peaks at 0.25 t, and ast is 0.5 − sin(saa) × (0.25 − sea). To obtain the sea, we can use the height (h) of a vertical rod on a level surface and the length (L) of its shadow: sea = atan2(h, L).

In addition to determining the sea, the atan2 function can also be used to calculate ast, as shown in Equation 30, where j is a latitude measured in turns called jīng (j). In Dec, inverse trigonometric functions like atan2 yield t as opposed to rad. At the Equator (j = 0), the latitude is zero and Equation 30 simplifies to ast = 0.5 + atan2(-sin(saa)cos(sea), sin(sea)).

\[ \text{ast} = 0.5 + \text{atan2}\!\!\left( \begin{aligned} & \text{-sin(saa)cos(sea),} \\ & \text{sin(sea)cos(j) } - \\ & \text{cos(saa)cos(sea)sin(j)} \end{aligned} \right) \tag{30}\]

\[ \text{ast} = \begin{cases} \text{saa} & \text{j} = 0.25 \\ \text{atan2(-sin(saa)cos(sea), sin(sea))} & \text{j} = 0 \\ (1.5 - \text{saa}) \bmod 1 & \text{j} = -0.25 \end{cases} \tag{31}\]

At the North Pole (j = 0.25), the latitude is 0.25 j and Equation 30 simplifies to ast = saa. At the South Pole (j = -0.25), the latitude is -0.25 j and Equation 30 simplifies to ast = 0.5 − saa before solar noon or ast = 1.5 − saa after solar noon. In between the Equator and the South or North Pole (-0.25 < j < 0 ∨ 0 < j < 0.25), we should use Equation 17, 30, or 32.

\[ \text{ast} = 0.5 + \text{arccos}\!\!\left(\dfrac{\text{sin(sea)} - \text{sin(j)sin(sda)}}{\text{cos(j)cos(sda)}}\right) \ast \begin{cases} \text{-}1 & 0 \le \text{saa} < 0.5 \\ 1 & 0.5 \le \text{saa} < 1 \end{cases} \tag{32}\]

Dec uses the term “minuend expansion” to describe the replacement of a minuend with a subtrahend and a difference. We can replace the current tod with the sum of either zero and a “positive interval tod” (pit), which is the time since the start of the current day (the previous midnight), or 1 day (d) and a “negative interval tod” (nit), which is the negative of the time until the start of the next day (the next midnight), as shown in Equation 34 below. Dec is not the first system to use decimal time. During the French Revolution, decimal time was mandatory for French public records for 197 days (d), from 1794+205 to 1795+037. On 1998+247, the Swatch corporation announced Swatch Internet Time, which divides the day into 1000 “.beats”, only has one time zone, and is displayed by Dec as tod−β.

Declock measures time in fractional days. A single decimal tod replaces the three parts of an hms triplet and can be shown at any precision by changing its metric prefix. With one b9 digit and one b24 digit after four b10 digits, a tod can match any hms triplet exactly, and a uto keeps Dec in step with ut without leap seconds. Dec derives its ten tzo from longitude and never uses negative offsets, but it lets anyone write any sto after a tod without forcing them to change it. With the eot and sda, we can turn a Zone 0 tod into solar time and estimate the tod of sunrise, solar noon, and sunset. Finally, minuend expansion turns any tod into a countdown or countup relative to an event, such as midnight, sunset, or the start of the year.

# Annual day aggregate (ada) {#sec-ada .hiddenheading}

The red line indicates a "day of year" ([doy](#doy){#dayofyear .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day of year"}): \${selDateHsl()}, which identifies a day in a year like a [Gregorian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar#:~:text=the%20calendar%20used%20in%20most%20parts%20of%20the%20world) month and "day of month" ([dom](#dom){#dayofmonth .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day of month"}). The red dot denotes a "time of day" ([tod](#tod){#timeofday .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}): \${selTimeDay()}, which specifies a point in a day like an "hour minute second" ([hms](#hms){#hourminutesecond .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="hour minute second"}) triplet. Together, a [doy](#doy){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day of year"} and [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} can form an "annual day aggregate" ([ada](#ada){#annualdayaggregate .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="annual day aggregate"}): \${selDateHsl()} + \${selTimeDay()} &div; 1000 = \${selDatePadHsl()}.\${selTimeMilDay()}.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup00}
$$
\begin{split}
\text{doy} &= \lfloor\text{ada}\rfloor \\
&= \text{ada} - \text{tod}
\end{split}
$$ {#eq-ada}
:::

As their names suggest, [doy](#doy){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="days of year"} and [ada](#ada){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="annual day aggregates"} are measured in days. The [measurement unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_measurement#:~:text=a%20definite%20magnitude%20of%20a%20quantity) of a [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} can be a day or a [submultiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_%28mathematics%29#Submultiple:~:text=of%20%22a%20being-,a%20unit%20fraction,-of%20b%22%20) of a day with a metric prefix. By changing how a decimal [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} is measured, we can shift its [decimal separator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_separator#:~:text=a%20symbol%20that%20separates%20the%20integer%20part%20from%20the%20fractional%20part%20of%20a%20number) or turn it into an integer. The [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="times of day"} along the y-axis of the area chart are integers because they have three digits and are measured in [millidays]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="thousandths of a day"}.

# Epochal day aggregate (eda) {#sec-eda .hiddenheading}

To obtain a [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} from a [ada](#ada){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="annual day aggregate"}, we can [keep the remainder after dividing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo#:~:text=returns%20the%20remainder) by one to isolate the [decimal part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_part#:~:text=the%20excess%20beyond%20that%20number%27s%20integer%20part) of the [quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient#:~:text=a%20quantity%20produced%20by%20the%20division%20of%20two%20numbers): \${zeroDotyHsl()}.\${zeroTimeHsl()} [mod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo#:~:text=returns%20the%20remainder) 1 = [0]{.color0}.\${zeroTimeHsl()}. We can use this same approach to separate a [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} from an "epochal day aggregate" ([eda](#eda){#epochaldayaggregate .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="epochal day aggregate"}): \${zeroDoteHsl}.\${zeroTimeHsl()} mod 1 = [0]{.color0}.\${zeroTimeHsl()}. The current [eda](#eda){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="epochal day aggregate"} tells us how many days have passed since the Dec [epoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch#:~:text=an%20instant%20in%20time%20chosen%20as%20the%20origin%20of%20a%20particular%20calendar%20era): Year [0]{.color0} Day [0]{.color0} [Tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} [0]{.color0}.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup01}
$$\begin{split}
\text{tod} &= \text{ada} \bmod 1 \\
&= \text{eda} \bmod 1
\end{split}$$ {#eq-tod}
:::

## UNIX time equation {#sec-ute .unnumbered .hiddenheading}

Similarly, [UNIX time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unix_time#:~:text=the%20number%20of%20non%2Dleap%20seconds%20that%20have%20elapsed%20since%2000%3A00%3A00%20UTC%20on%201%C2%A0January%201970%2C%20the%20Unix%20epoch) tallies the seconds since the [UNIX epoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unix_time#:~:text=00%3A00%3A00%20UTC%20on%201%C2%A0January%201970), which is exactly 719468 days after the Dec epoch. To get the [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} in Zone [0]{.color0}, the Dec time zone that is in between the two leftmost vertical lines on the map, we can divide UNIX time by the [number of seconds in a day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day#:~:text=average%2C%20this%20is-,24%20hours%20(86%2C400%20seconds),-.%20As%20a%20day) and then keep the remainder after dividing the resulting days by 1 as in @eq-unixtod.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup02}
$$\text{tod} = \text{unix} \div 86400 \bmod 1$$ {#eq-unixtod}
:::

## Julian date equation {#sec-jde .unnumbered .hiddenheading}

[Julian dates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#:~:text=the%20Julian%20day%20number%20plus%20the%20fraction%20of%20a%20day%20since%20the%20preceding%20noon) track the days since the beginning of the [Julian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#:~:text=a%20chronological%20interval%20of%207980%C2%A0years%2C%20derived%20from%20three%20multi%2Dyear%20cycles:%20the%20Indiction%2C%20Solar%2C%20and%20Lunar%20cycles) and thus are akin to [eda](#eda){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="epochal day aggregates"}. We can produce a Zone [5]{.color5} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} from a [Julian date](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_day#:~:text=the%20Julian%20day%20number%20plus%20the%20fraction%20of%20a%20day%20since%20the%20preceding%20noon) simply by keeping the remainder after dividing by one. If we want a Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} instead, we should add [5]{.color5} [decidays]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="tenths of a day"} to the Julian date, divide the sum by one, and then keep the remainder as in @eq-juliantod below.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup03}
$$\text{tod} = (\text{julian} + 0.5) \bmod 1$$ {#eq-juliantod}
:::

# Hour minute second (hms) {#sec-hms .hiddenheading}

We can also obtain a Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} from a [Coordinated Universal Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinated_Universal_Time#:~:text=the%20primary%20time%20standard%20globally%20used%20to%20regulate%20clocks%20and%20time) ([UTC](#utc){#coordinateduniversaltime .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"}) [hms](#hms){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="hour minute second"} triplet by summing its components after converting them to fractional days, as shown in the equation below. The [computer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming#:~:text=the%20composition%20of%20sequences%20of%20instructions%2C%20called%20programs%2C%20that%20computers%20can%20follow%20to%20perform%20tasks) code in the [tabset panel](https://quarto.org/docs/interactive/layout.html#tabset-panel) beneath the equation compares [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="times of day"} derived from [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} and UNIX time as Quarto was [rendering](https://quarto.org/docs/get-started/hello/rstudio.html#rendering:~:text=When%20rendering%2C%20Quarto%20generates%20a%20new%20file%20that%20contains%20selected%20text%2C%20code%2C%20and%20results) this webpage.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup04}
$$\text{tod} = \dfrac{\text{hour}}{24} + \dfrac{\text{minute}}{1440} + \dfrac{\text{second}}{86400}$$ {#eq-hmstod}
:::

# Universal time offset (uto) {#sec-uto .hiddenheading}

The [Global Positioning System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System#:~:text=a%20satellite%2Dbased%20hyperbolic%20navigation%20system), [BeiDou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BeiDou#:~:text=a%20satellite%2Dbased%20radio%20navigation%20system), and [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_(satellite_navigation)#:~:text=a%20global%20navigation%20satellite%20system%20(GNSS)%20created%20by%20the%20European%20Union) [global navigation satellite systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation#:~:text=a%20satellite%20navigation%20system%20with%20global%20coverage) along with most --- if not all --- [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_language#:~:text=a%20system%20of%20notation%20for%20writing%20source%20code%20such%20as%20used%20to%20produce%20a%20computer%20program) do not account for [leap seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_second#:~:text=one%2Dsecond%20adjustment), which appears to be for the best given that leap seconds [can cause problems](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/leap-seconds-faqs#:~:text=detriments%20to%20adding%20leap%20seconds) and will be abolished no later than [2027]{.color27by100}+[081]{.color081by366} according to the [International Bureau of Weights and Measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Bureau_of_Weights_and_Measures#:~:text=an%20intergovernmental%20organisation%20(IGO)%2C%20through%20which%20its%2067%20member%2Dstates%20act%20on%20measurement%20standards).

On or before the deadline to abolish leap seconds, the tolerance for disagreement between [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} and [Universal Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Time#Versions:~:text=a%20time%20standard%20based%20on%20Earth%27s%20rotation) ([UT](#ut){#universaltime .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Universal Time"}) will increase from [1 Dec second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_second#:~:text=to%20ensure%20that%20the%20difference%20between%20the%20UTC%20and%20UT1%20readings%20will%20never%20exceed%200.9%20seconds) ([s](#s){#second .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec seconds"}) to 3750 [s](#s){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec seconds"}. Since [1972]{.color72by100}+[122]{.color122by365}, only [27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_second#:~:text=27%20leap%20seconds%20have%20been%20added%20to%20UTC) leap seconds have been inserted, which is 0.75% of 3750 [s](#s){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec seconds"}, and at this rate it will take over five millennia for the disparity between [UT](#ut){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Universal Time"} and [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} to reach 3750 [s](#s){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec seconds"}.

Instead of leap seconds, Dec matches [UT](#ut){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Universal Time"} using a "[universal time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Time#:~:text=a%20time%20standard%20based%20on%20Earth%27s%20rotation) [offset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTC_offset#:~:text=the%20difference%20in%20hours%20and%20minutes%20between%20Coordinated%20Universal%20Time%20(UTC)%20and%20the%20standard%20time%20at%20a%20particular%20place)" ([uto](#uto){#universaltimeoffset .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="universal time offset"}). With the [leap second insertion dates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_second#:~:text=Announced%20leap%20seconds%20to%20date) provided by the [International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Earth_Rotation_and_Reference_Systems_Service#:~:text=the%20body%20responsible%20for%20maintaining%20global%20time%20and%20reference%20frame%20standards), we can approximate the [uto](#uto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="universal time offset"} that yields [UT](#ut){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Universal Time"} when added to the Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} on the [Dec date](/dec/date) chosen by the [range](https://observablehq.com/framework/inputs/range)🎚️inputs below: \${leapCountHsl} &div; 8640 = \${leapSdecHsl()}\${leapSb24hsl()} &div; 90000 = \${leapTzoHsl()}.

# Second interval difference (sid) {#sec-sid .hiddenheading}

The equation above divides leap seconds by the number of [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units#:~:text=the%20world%27s%20most%20widely%20used%20system%20of%20measurement) ([SI](#si){#internationalsystemofunits .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="International System of Units"}) seconds ([sis](#sis){#sisecond .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="SI seconds"}) or Dec [deciseconds]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="tenths of a second"} ([ds](#ds){#decisecond .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec deciseconds"}) in a [deciday]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="tenth of a day"} ([dd](#dd){#deciday .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="deciday"}): 8640 [sis](#sis){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="SI seconds"} = 90000 [ds](#ds){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec deciseconds"} = 1 [dd](#dd){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="deciday"}. To avoid [repeating decimals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repeating_decimal#:~:text=the%20same%20sequence%20of%20digits%20is%20repeated%20forever) when turning [sis](#sis){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="SI seconds"} into [ds](#ds){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec deciseconds"}, we can put a Dec [quadravigesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_numeral_systems#:~:text=24-,Quadravigesimal,-%5B48%5D) ([base24]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="quadravigesimal"}) digit in place of the ones digit and decimal part of the [ds](#ds){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec deciseconds"}: \${leapSdecHsl()}\${leapSb24hsl()} = \${leapSdecHsl()} &times; 10 + \${leapSsidHsl} &div; 2.4.

Known as a "second interval difference" ([sid](#sid){#secondintervaldifference .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="second interval difference"}), the [base24]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="quadravigesimal"} ([b24](#b24){#base24 .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"}) character at the end of \${leapSdecHsl()}\${leapSb24hsl()} is a letter of the [Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet#:~:text=the%20writing%20system%20used%20for%20the%20Greek%20language). To display [sis](#sis){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="SI seconds"} in [ds](#ds){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec deciseconds"}, we can concatenate the integer part of the [s](#s){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec seconds"} we obtain from @eq-sis with the [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"} representation of the [sid](#sid){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="second interval difference"} we get from @eq-sid. The table beneath these equations lists [base10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal#:~:text=a%20numeral%20system%20that%20uses%20ten%20as%20its%20radix%20%28base%29) ([b10](#b10){#base10 .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base10"}) and [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"} equivalents.

# Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) {#sec-utc .hiddenheading}

Rather than use a terminator as in @tbl-time, Dec expresses one hour in [dd](#dd){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="decidays"} as a single [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"} digit: 0.41[♄]{.color6by9 .mono} = [β]{.color1by24 .mono}. If we add [β]{.color1by24 .mono} [dd](#dd){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="deciday"} to the Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}, we get the [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} associated with the [UTC+1 offset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTC%2B01:00#:~:text=a%20time%20offset%20from%20UTC%20of%20%2B01%3A00): [0]{.color0}.\${zeroTimeHsl()} + [β]{.color1by24 .mono} &div; 10 = [0]{.color0}.\${utc1timeHsl()}. Currently, the Zone [0]{.color0} Dec time is [0]{.color0}.\${zeroTimeHsl()}&minus;[0]{.color0} and the UTC+1 Dec time is [0]{.color0}.\${utc1timeHsl()}&minus;[β]{.color1by24 .mono}. Dec times consist of a [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}, a minus sign (&minus;), and an offset.

There are 12 [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} offsets that become a terminating decimals after being converted from hours to days and therefore could be displayed in either [b10](#b10){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base10"} or [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"}. Although it has only ten time zones, Dec can express any [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} offset using one or two non-negative [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"} digits. There are 27 [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} offsets that require only one [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"} digit and 11 that need two [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"} digits.

Out of the [38](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_UTC_offsets) [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} offsets, 14 are negative (&lt;[UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"}) and 24 are non-negative (&ge;[UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"}). The [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} time zone with [the most negative offset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTC%E2%88%9212:00#:~:text=a%20nautical%20time%20zone%20comprising%20the%20high%20seas%20between%20180%C2%B0%20and%20172%C2%B030%E2%80%B2W%20longitude) is completely uninhabited. The bar chart below visualizes the population of each [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} time zone based on 2020 [Socioeconomic Data and Applications Center](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/centers/sedac-daac) data. The vast majority of all people live in [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} time zones with &ge;[UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} offsets.

Negative [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} offsets only exist in [the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas#:~:text=a%20landmass%20comprising%20the%20totality%20of%20North%20America%20and%20South%20America) and islands in the [Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_islands_in_the_Atlantic_Ocean) and [Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_islands_in_the_Pacific_Ocean) Oceans. Therefore, the bar chart above is essentially comparing the populations of the Americas and the rest of the world. According to 2021 [United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Department_of_Economic_and_Social_Affairs#:~:text=part%20of%20the%20United%20Nations%20Secretariat) data, roughly 1 billion out of a total of almost 8 billion people live in the Americas.

Dec forbids the association of dates with negative offsets. Therefore, Dec will add ten [decidays]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="tenths of a day"} to any negative offset and add one day to the date associated with that offset. Negative offset handling has no effect on the [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} or the conversion of an [hms](#hms){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="hour minute second"} triplet to a [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} and is typically only necessary after a "year month day" ([ymd](#ymd){#yearmonthday .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="year month day"}) triplet is converted into a Dec date.

If we do know whether Day [364]{.color364by365} belongs to a common or leap year, adding one day to it yields either Day [0]{.color0} or Day [365]{.color365by366} and this uncertainty is expressed as a [multiplication sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_sign) over the last digit of [36[5]{.acutegrave}]{.color0}. The sign hints that [36[5]{.acutegrave}]{.color0} is short for [365]{.color0}&times;`leap`, where [36[5]{.acutegrave}]{.color0} is either [365]{.color365by366} if `leap` is 1 or [0]{.color0} if `leap` is 0. We can find `leap` by passing any year ([y](#y){#year .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="year"}) to @eq-leap below.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup07}
$$
\text{leap}=\begin{cases}
  1&{\begin{aligned}
    &\text{ if } (\text{y} + 1)\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo#:~:text=returns%20the%20remainder%20or%20signed%20remainder%20of%20a%20division}{\bmod} \ \ \ \ 4=0\\
    &\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_conjunction}{\land}(\text{y} + 1)\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo#:~:text=returns%20the%20remainder%20or%20signed%20remainder%20of%20a%20division}{\bmod} 100\neq0\\
    &\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_disjunction}{\lor}(\text{y} + 1)\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo#:~:text=returns%20the%20remainder%20or%20signed%20remainder%20of%20a%20division}{\bmod} 400=0\end{aligned}}\\\\
  0&{\text{ otherwise}}\end{cases}
$$ {#eq-leap}

# Day of week (dow) {#sec-dow .hiddenheading}

Adding one day to the [doy](#doy){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day of year"} has no effect on the [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}, but it increments the [dom](#dom){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day of month"} and "day of week" ([dow](#dow){#dayofweek .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day of week"}). Dec meets the expectations of [Gregorian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar#:~:text=the%20calendar%20currently%20used%20in%20most%20parts%20of%20the%20world) users who live in [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} time zones with negative offsets by essentially deriving the previous [dom](#dom){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="days of month"} or [dow](#dow){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="days of week"} from the current [doy](#doy){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day of year"} if a location in the Americas is provided without an offset or with a negative offset.

# Time zone offset (tzo) {#sec-tzo .hiddenheading}

In Dec, "time zone offsets" ([tzo](#tzo){#timezoneoffset .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time zone offsets"}) are derived solely from longitude. Dec measures [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude#:~:text=denoted%20by%20the%20Greek%20letter%20lambda) in [wěi](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E7%B7%AF#:~:text=(geography)-,latitude,-coordinate%20terms%C2%A0%E2%96%B2) ([w](#w){#wei .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="horizontal lines on a map"}), arcbeats ([a](#a){#arcbeat .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="arcbeats"}), or submultiples thereof. If we travel East or West until we reach our starting point, we will cover 1 [w](#w){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="horizontal line on a map"} or 100000 ([10^5^]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="a hundred thousand"}) [a](#a){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="arcbeats"}. A [tzo](#tzo){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time zone offsets"} is essentially a [deciwěi]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="tenth of a wěi"} ([dw](#dw){#deciwei .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="deciwěi"}) longitude that became a single-digit [b10](#b10){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base10"} integer via  [truncation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncation#Truncation_and_floor_function:~:text=truncation%20is%20limiting%20the%20number%20of%20digits%20right%20of%20the%20decimal%20point) or [flooring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_and_ceiling_functions#:~:text=takes%20as%20input%20a%20real%20number%20x%2C%20and%20gives%20as%20output%20the%20greatest%20integer%20less%20than%20or%20equal%20to%20x): &LeftFloor;\${longInputHsl} &div; 100&RightFloor; = \${longFloorHsl}.

The [Equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator#:~:text=the%20circle%20of%20latitude%20that%20divides%20Earth%20into%20the%20Northern%20and%20Southern%20Hemispheres) is approximately ([~]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="approximately"}) 1 [taur]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="circumference = 𝜏r"} ([c](#c){#taur .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="taur"}) or [~]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="approximately"}[10^5^]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="a hundred thousand"} [kilozem]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="thousands of zone equatorial meters"} ([kz](#kz){#kilozem .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="thousands of zone equatorial meters"}) long. Near the Equator, a longitude that has a [precision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_degrees#Precision) of 0.1 [microwěi]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="millionths of wěi"} ([&micro;w](#uw){#microwei .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="microwěi"}) or 10 milliarcbeats ([ma](#ma){#milliarcbeats .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="milliarcbeats"}) is accurate to within [~]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="approximately"}0.1 [microtaur]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="millionths of taur"} ([&micro;c](#uc){#microtaur .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="microtaur"}), [~]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="approximately"}10 "zone equatorial meters" ([zem]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="zone equatorial meters"}), or 4 [meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre#Definition:~:text=the%20base%20unit%20of%20length%20in%20the%20International%20System%20of%20Units), which is about the width of a [U-shaped living room layout](https://www.dimensions.com/collection/living-room-layouts) or the length of a typical [subcompact car](https://www.dimensions.com/collection/subcompact-cars-b-segment).

For precise [geopositioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopositioning#:~:text=estimating%20the%20geographic%20position%20of%20an%20object%20or%20a%20person), we may want to express [geographic coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_coordinate_system#:~:text=for%20measuring%20and%20communicating%20positions%20directly%20on%20Earth%20as%20latitude%20and%20longitude) in [ma](#ma){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="milliarcbeats"}, but we are unlikely to need [s](#s){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Dec seconds"} or [cmd](#cmd){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="centimillidays"} when displaying the [solar time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time#:~:text=a%20calculation%20of%20the%20passage%20of%20time%20based%20on%20the%20position%20of%20the%20Sun%20in%20the%20sky) or estimates of what the [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} will be when the Sun rises, reaches its [highest point of the day](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noon#:~:text=highest%20position%20above%20the%20horizon), or sets. By default, Dec uses [md](#md){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="millidays"} precision to display a solar time and the "solar time offset" ([sto](#sto){#solartimeoffset .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar time offset"}) used to produce it.

In Dec, any offset can be called a [sto](#sto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar time offset"} or a [uto](#uto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="universal time offset"}, because there will always be a longitude at which the sum of the given offset and the Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} will yield solar time or [UT](#ut){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Universal Time"}. In contrast, Dec has only ten [tzo](#tzo){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time zone offsets"}, one for each of its ten time zones. Dec translates [UTC](#utc){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Coordinated Universal Time"} to Zone [0]{.color0} and UTC&minus;12 or UTC+12 to Zone [5]{.color5}, but does not categorize any other UTC offset as a [tzo](#tzo){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time zone offset"}.

Dec permits any non-negative offset that is less than one day and can be written with a finite number of [b9](#b9){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base9"}, [b10](#b10){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base10"}, and [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"} digits. Dec times represent truncated math expressions that simplify to the Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}: [0]{.color0}.\${utc3timeHsl()}&minus;[125]{.color3by24}&div;1000 = [0]{.color0}.\${utc3timeHsl()}&minus;[δ]{.color3by24 .mono}&div;10 = [0]{.color0}.\${zeroTimeHsl()}. The [UTC+3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTC%2B03:00#:~:text=a%20time%20offset%20from%20UTC%20of%20%2B03%3A00) [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} is the sum of the Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} and either [δ]{.color3by24 .mono} [dd](#dd){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="decidays"} or [125]{.color3by24} [md](#md){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="millidays"}. Therefore, the current [UTC+3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTC%2B03:00#:~:text=a%20time%20offset%20from%20UTC%20of%20%2B03%3A00) Dec time can be either [0]{.color0}.\${utc3timeHsl()}&minus;[δ]{.color3by24 .mono} or [0]{.color0}.\${utc3timeHsl()}&minus;[125]{.color3by24}.

# Solar time offset (sto) {#sec-sto .hiddenheading}

China is a good example of the difference between a [sto](#sto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar time offset"} and a [tzo](#tzo){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time zone offset"}. The territory of China spans three Dec time zones, Zones [2]{.color2}, [3]{.color3}, and [4]{.color4}, but the government of China has decided that everyone in China should use UTC+08:00, which is Earth ([♁]{.color3by9}) in [b9](#b9){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base9"} or iota ([ι]{.color8by24}) in [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"}. Dec does not enforce the use of any offset, but it would suggest that users in China consider a [tzo](#tzo){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time zone offset"} of [3]{.color3} [dd](#dd){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="decidays"}, which is not far off from 8/24, 1/3, [♁]{.color3by9}, or [ι]{.color8by24}. Zone [3]{.color3} may actually be closer to the [sto](#sto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar time offset"} of most people in China, because the center of Zone [3]{.color3}, 350 [mw](#mw){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="milliwěi"}, is close to the geographic center of China, [~]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="approximately"}337 [mw](#mw){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="milliwěi"}, whereas the central longitude of UTC+08:00, [~]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="approximately"}383 [mw](#mw){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="milliwěi"}, is much further East.

To get a local solar time, we add a [sto](#sto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar time offset"} to a Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}. If the [sto](#sto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar time offset"} accounts only for longitude, the result is an [mst](#mst){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="mean solar time"}. If it also includes the [eot](#eot){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="equation of time"}, the result is an [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"}. Whereas a Dec time zone spans a [dw](#dw){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="deciwěi"}, a [sto](#sto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar time offset"} represents a single longitude. In @eq-mst below, the [sto](#sto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar time offset"} of a longitude in [w](#w){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="horizontal lines on a map"} is (0.95 + [w](#w){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="horizontal lines on a map"}) mod 1.

# Equation of time (eot) {#sec-eot .hiddenheading}

The two types of [solar time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time#:~:text=a%20calculation%20of%20the%20passage%20of%20time%20based%20on%20the%20position%20of%20the%20Sun%20in%20the%20sky) are ["mean solar time"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time#Mean_solar_time:~:text=it%20follows%20an%20imaginary%20%22mean%20Sun%22%20that%20moves%20along%20the%20celestial%20equator%20at%20a%20constant%20rate%20that%20matches%20the%20real%20Sun%27s%20average%20rate%20over%20the%20year) ([mst](#mst){#meansolartime .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="mean solar time"}) and ["apparent solar time"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time#Apparent_solar_time:~:text=the%20apparent%20motion%20of%20the%20actual%20Sun) ([ast](#ast){#apparentsolartime .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"}). To calculate an [mst](#mst){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="mean solar time"}, we keep only the decimal part of the sum of 0.95, the Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} measured in days, and a longitude measured in [w](#w){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="horizontal lines on a map"}. If we want [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"} instead of [mst](#mst){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="mean solar time"}, the sum needs to include the result of plugging the "time of year" ([toy](#toy){#timeofyear .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of year"}) into the ["equation of time"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation_of_time#:~:text=the%20discrepancy%20between%20two%20kinds%20of%20solar%20time) ([eot](#eot){#equationoftime .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="equation of time"}).

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup09}
$$\text{syl} = \text{leap} + 365$$ {#eq-syl}

$$\text{toy} = \text{ada} \div \text{syl}$$ {#eq-toy}

$$\text{mst} = (0.95 + \text{tod} + \text{w}) \bmod 1$$ {#eq-mst}

$$\text{ast} = (0.95 + \text{tod} + \text{w} +  \text{eot}) \bmod 1$$ {#eq-ast}
:::

To obtain a [toy](#toy){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of year"}, we divide an [ada](#ada){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="annual day aggregate"} by a "solar year length" ([syl](#syl){#solaryearlength .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar year length"}), which is the number of days in the year. Dec passes the [toy](#toy){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of year"} directly from @eq-toy to the [eot](#eot){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="equation of time"} function in @eq-eot without first multiplying it by 2𝜋 or 𝜏 to convert it from [turns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28angle%29#:~:text=a%20unit%20of%20plane%20angle%20measurement%20equal%20to%202%CF%80%C2%A0radians%2C%20360%C2%A0degrees) ([t](#t){#turn .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="turns"}) to [radians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radian#:~:text=the%20unit%20of%20angle%20in%20the%20International%20System%20of%20Units) ([rad](#rad){#radian .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="radians"}) because the [sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_and_cosine#:~:text=the%20ratio%20of%20the%20length%20of%20the%20side%20opposite%20that%20angle%20to%20the%20length%20of%20the%20longest%20side%20of%20the%20triangle) and [cos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_and_cosine#:~:text=the%20ratio%20of%20the%20length%20of%20the%20adjacent%20leg%20to%20that%20of%20the%20hypotenuse) [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_functions#:~:text=functions%20which%20relate%20an%20angle%20of%20a%20right%2Dangled%20triangle%20to%20ratios%20of%20two%20side%20lengths) used by Dec are designed for [t](#t){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="turns"} instead of [rad](#rad){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="radians"}.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup10}
$$\begin{split}
\text{eot} & = \beta_0 \\
& + \beta_1 \ast \text{cos(toy)} \\
& + \beta_2 \ast \text{sin(toy)} \\
& + \beta_3 \ast \text{cos(2} \ast \text{toy)} \\
& + \beta_4 \ast \text{sin(2} \ast \text{toy)}
\end{split}$$ {#eq-eot}
:::

By comparing the `sincost` and `sincos` functions, the code below demonstrates that using [t](#t){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="turns"} rather than [rad](#rad){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="radians"} can [yield more accurate results](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_and_cosine#:~:text=Representing%20angles%20in%20turns%20or%20half%2Dturns%20has%20accuracy%20advantages). Whereas `sincost` expects to receive [t](#t){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="turns"} and is always correct, `sincos` assumes that it will get [rad](#rad){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="radians"} and generates the slightly incorrect [E notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_notation#E_notation) values below because it cannot produce zero unless its input is zero.

Therefore, [mst](#mst){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="mean solar time"} and [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"} are equivalent four times per year.

Apart from transforming an [mst](#mst){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="mean solar time"} into an [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"}, we can also use [eot](#eot){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="equation of time"} values to more accurately estimate the [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="times of day"} of [solar noon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noon#:~:text=reaching%20its%20highest%20position%20above%20the%20horizon%20on%20that%20day%20and%20casting%20the%20shortest%20shadow), sunrise, and sunset. @eq-solarnoon below creates a [solar noon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noon#:~:text=reaching%20its%20highest%20position%20above%20the%20horizon%20on%20that%20day%20and%20casting%20the%20shortest%20shadow) [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} by keeping only the decimal part of the result of subtracting a [w](#w){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="horizontal lines on a map"} longitude and an [eot](#eot){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="equation of time"} value that is measured in days from the sum of 9.55 and a [tzo](#tzo){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time zone offset"} that is measured in days.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup11}
$$\text{solarnoon} = \left(9.55 + \text{tzo} - \text{w} - \text{eot}\right) \bmod 1$$ {#eq-solarnoon}
:::

# Cambridge and Cambridge {#sec-cac .hiddenheading}

To compare the solar noon [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} in two cities, we can plug the longitude of each city into @eq-solarnoon above. If all of the variables other than longitude in @eq-solarnoon are set to zero, the resulting solar noon [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} is almost five [decidays]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="tenths of a day"} for [Cambridge, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge#:~:text=a%20city%20and%20non%2Dmetropolitan%20district%20in%20the%20county%20of%20Cambridgeshire%2C%20England) in the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom#:~:text=a%20country%20in%20Northwestern%20Europe) and nearly seven [decidays]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="tenths of a day"} for [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts#:~:text=a%20city%20in%20Middlesex%20County%2C%20Massachusetts%2C%20United%20States) in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States#:~:text=a%20country%20primarily%20located%20in%20North%20America).

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup12}
$$4.99635 = (9.55 - 0.050365) \bmod 1 \ast 10$$ {#eq-cambridgeuk}

$$6.97517 = (9.55 - 0.852484) \bmod 1 \ast 10$$ {#eq-cambridgema}
:::

The two homonymous cities are about two [dw](#dw){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="deciwěi"} apart and therefore will always differ by around two [decidays]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="tenths of a day"} in solar time, regardless of what time zone we use as our frame of reference. England is in Zone [0]{.color0} and Massachusetts is in Zone [8]{.color8}. From the perspective of Zone [8]{.color8}, solar noon is roughly [Tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} [5]{.color5} in Cambridge, MA and about [Tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} [3]{.color3} in Cambridge, UK.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup13}
$$2.99635 = (9.55 + 0.8 - 0.050365) \bmod 1 \ast 10$$ {#eq-cambridgeuk8}

$$4.97517 = (9.55 + 0.8 - 0.852484) \bmod 1 \ast 10$$ {#eq-cambridgema8}
:::

# Light arc whole (law) {#sec-law .hiddenheading}

The path in the sky that the Sun appears to follow from a sunrise to a sunset is a "light [arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_path#:~:text=arc%2Dlike%20path%20that%20the%20Sun%20appears%20to%20follow%20across%20the%20sky) whole" ([law](#law){#lightarcwhole .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"}). Solar noon, the midpoint of a [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"}, is halfway between sunrise and sunset. A [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"} can be evenly split into two "light arc halves" ([lah](#lah){#lightarchalf .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc half"}). Whereas [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="times of day"} are time points, [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc wholes"} and [lah](#lah){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc halves"} are durations. The difference between a solar noon [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} and a [lah](#lah){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc half"} is a sunrise [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup14}
$$\text{law} = \text{lah} \ast 2$$ {#eq-law}

$$\begin{split}
\text{sunrise}
& = \text{solarmidnight} + \text{nah} \\
& = \text{solarnoon} - \text{lah} \\
& = \text{sunset} - \text{law}
\end{split}$$ {#eq-sunrise}

$$\begin{split}
\text{sunset}
& = \text{solarmidnight} + \text{nah} + \text{law} \\
& = \text{sunrise} + \text{law} \\
& = \text{solarnoon} + \text{lah} \\
\end{split}$$ {#eq-sunset}
:::

# Night arc whole (naw) {#sec-naw .unnumbered .hiddenheading}

The Sun continues its path after it disappears below the horizon, moving from sunset to sunrise along a "night arc whole" ([naw](#naw){#nightarcwhole .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="night arc whole"}). Like [conjugate angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle#:~:text=conjugate%20angles%20sum%20to%20a%20full%20angle), a [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"} and a [naw](#naw){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="night arc whole"} form a full circle that represents one day. Likewise, a [lah](#lah){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc half"} and a "night arc half" ([nah](#nah){#nightarchalf .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="night arc half"}) are like two [supplementary angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle#:~:text=sum%20to%20a%20straight%20angle) that form a semicircle from solar noon to the [naw](#naw){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="night arc whole"} midpoint: [solar midnight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midnight#:~:text=the%20time%20opposite%20to%20solar%20noon%2C%20when%20the%20Sun%20is%20closest%20to%20the%20nadir%2C%20and%20the%20night%20is%20equidistant%20from%20dusk%20and%20dawn).

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup15}
$$\text{naw} = \text{nah} \ast 2$$ {#eq-naw}

$$\begin{split}
\text{day}
& = \text{law} + \text{naw} \\
& = (\text{lah} + \text{nah}) \ast 2
\end{split}$$ {#eq-day}

$$\begin{split}
\text{solarmidnight}
&= \text{sunrise} - \text{nah} \\
&= \text{solarnoon} - \text{lah} - \text{nah} \\
&= \text{sunset} - \text{law} - \text{nah}
\end{split}$$ {#eq-solarmidnight}
:::

The range input below controls the yellow [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"} and the blue [naw](#naw){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="night arc whole"} in the diagram beneath it. At the Equator, the sun always rises about a quarter turn from North and sets around three quarter turns from North, resulting in a [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"} of roughly half a day: 75% &minus; 25% = 50%. If the [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"} is zero, a [polar night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_night#:~:text=the%20Sun%20remains%20below%20the%20horizon%20for%20more%20than%2024%C2%A0hours) occurs. A day without a [naw](#naw){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="night arc whole"} is called a [polar day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midnight_sun#:~:text=the%20Sun%20remains%20visible%20at%20the%20local%20midnight).

{{< include /dec/time/_dayarcplot.qmd >}}

The numbers in the diagram above are ["solar azimuth angle"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_azimuth_angle#:~:text=horizontal%20angle%20with%20respect%20to%20north) ([saa](#saa){#solarazimuthangle .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar azimuth angle"}) [milliturns]{.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="thousandths of a turn"} ([mt](#mt){#milliturn .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="milliturns"}). At solar noon, the [saa](#saa){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar azimuth angle"} is 0 [mt](#mt){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="milliturns"} in the [Southern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere#:~:text=the%20half%20%28hemisphere%29%20of%20Earth%20that%20is%20south%20of%20the%20equator) and 500 [mt](#mt){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="milliturns"} in the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere#:~:text=the%20half%20of%20Earth%20that%20is%20north%20of%20the%20equator). The [radio](https://observablehq.github.io/framework/inputs/radio#:~:text=allows%20the%20user%20to%20choose%20one%20of%20a%20given%20set%20of%20values)📻input above controls whether the midpoint of the [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"} is 0 or 500 [mt](#mt){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="milliturns"}. To measure the [saa](#saa){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar azimuth angle"} during the [daytime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytime#:~:text=the%20period%20of%20the%20day%20in%20which%20the%20Sun%20is%20above%20the%20local%20horizon%20in%20a%20given%20location), point a compass🧭at the spot on the horizon below the Sun.

During an [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox#:~:text=a%20moment%20in%20time%20when%20the%20Sun%20appears%20directly%20above%20the%20equator) at the Equator, the Sun goes from East to West directly overhead, the [saa](#saa){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar azimuth angle"} flips like a switch from 0.25 [t](#t){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="turns"} to 0.75 [t](#t){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="turns"} at solar noon, the "[solar elevation angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_zenith_angle#Summary_of_special_angles:~:text=For%20example%2C%20the-,solar,-elevation%20angle%20is)" ([sea](#sea){#solarelevationangle .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar elevation angle"}) peaks at 0.25 [t](#t){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="turns"}, and [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"} is 0.5 &minus; sin([saa](#saa){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar azimuth angle"}) &times; (0.25 &minus; [sea](#sea){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar elevation angle"}). To obtain the [sea](#sea){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar elevation angle"}, we can use the height (h) of a vertical rod on a level surface and the length (L) of its shadow: [sea](#sea){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar elevation angle"} = atan2(h, L).

In addition to determining the [sea](#sea){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar elevation angle"}, the  [atan2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atan2#:~:text=function%20atan2%20is-,the%202%2Dargument%20arctangent,-.%20By%20definition%2C) function can also be used to calculate [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"}, as shown in @eq-astjing, where [j](#j){#jing .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="jīng"} is a latitude measured in turns called [jīng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_arc#Full_meridian_(polar_perimeter):~:text=The%20polar%20Earth%27s%20circumference%20is%20simply%20four%20times%20quarter%20meridian) ([j](#j){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="jīng"}). In Dec, [inverse trigonometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_trigonometric_functions#:~:text=obtain%20an%20angle%20from%20any%20of%20the%20angle%27s%20trigonometric%20ratios) functions like [atan2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atan2#:~:text=function%20atan2%20is-,the%202%2Dargument%20arctangent,-.%20By%20definition%2C) yield [t](#t){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="turns"} as opposed to [rad](#rad){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="radians"}. At the Equator ([j](#j){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="jīng"} = 0), the latitude is zero and @eq-astjing simplifies to [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"} = 0.5 + atan2(-sin([saa](#saa){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar azimuth angle"})cos([sea](#sea){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar elevation angle"}), sin([sea](#sea){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar elevation angle"})).

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup16}
$$
\text{ast} = 0.5 + \text{atan2}\!\!\left(
\begin{aligned}
& \text{-sin(saa)cos(sea),} \\
& \text{sin(sea)cos(j) } - \\
& \text{cos(saa)cos(sea)sin(j)}
\end{aligned}
\right)
$$ {#eq-astjing}

$$
\text{ast} =
\begin{cases}
\text{saa} & \text{j} = 0.25 \\
\text{atan2(-sin(saa)cos(sea), sin(sea))} & \text{j} = 0 \\
(1.5 - \text{saa}) \bmod 1 & \text{j} = -0.25
\end{cases}
$$ {#eq-extremes}
:::

At the North Pole ([j](#j){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="jīng"} = 0.25), the latitude is 0.25 [j](#j){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="jīng"} and @eq-astjing simplifies to [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"} = [saa](#saa){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar azimuth angle"}. At the South Pole ([j](#j){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="jīng"} = -0.25), the latitude is -0.25 [j](#j){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="jīng"} and @eq-astjing simplifies to [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"} = 0.5 &minus; [saa](#saa){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar azimuth angle"} before solar noon or [ast](#ast){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="apparent solar time"} = 1.5 &minus; [saa](#saa){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar azimuth angle"} after solar noon. In between the Equator and the South or North Pole (-0.25 < [j](#j){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="jīng"} < 0 &or; 0 < [j](#j){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="jīng"} < 0.25), we should use Equation -@eq-ast, -@eq-astjing, or -@eq-astsha.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup17}
$$
\text{ast} = 0.5 + \text{arccos}\!\!\left(\dfrac{\text{sin(sea)} - \text{sin(j)sin(sda)}}{\text{cos(j)cos(sda)}}\right) \ast
\begin{cases}
\text{-}1 & 0 \le \text{saa} < 0.5 \\
1 & 0.5 \le \text{saa} < 1
\end{cases}
$$ {#eq-astsha}
:::

Unlike @eq-astjing, @eq-astsha requires a "solar declination angle" ([sda](#sda){#solardeclinationangle .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar declination angle"}). Whereas latitude is the angle from the Equator in the [geographic coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_coordinate_system#:~:text=a%20spherical%20or%20geodetic%20coordinate%20system%20for%20measuring%20and%20communicating%20positions%20directly%20on%20Earth%20as%20latitude%20and%20longitude), [declination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declination#:~:text=the%20angular%20distance%20from%20the%20celestial%20equator%20on%20the%20celestial%20sphere) is the angle from the [celestial equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_equator) in the [equatorial coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_coordinate_system#:~:text=a%20celestial%20coordinate%20system%20widely%20used%20to%20specify%20the%20positions%20of%20celestial%20objects). For simplicity, we can use @eq-eot for both the [eot](#eot){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="equation of time"} and [sda](#sda){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar declination angle"}. The equation above can be summarized as sunset &minus; sunrise = [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"} or [minuend](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/minuend#:~:text=A%20number%20or%20quantity%20from%20which%20another%20is%20to%20be%20subtracted) &minus; [subtrahend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtraction#:~:text=number%20being%20subtracted) = [difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtraction#Notation_and_terminology:~:text=The%20result%20is%20the%20difference). If we replace the minuend (sunset [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}) with the current [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} and the subtrahend (sunrise [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}) with the sunset [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}, the difference will no longer be the [law](#law){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="light arc whole"} but rather the time until sunset if it is negative or since sunset if it is positive: \${selectedCurrent.toFixed(3)} = \${selectedSunset.toFixed(3)} [${selectedDifference < 0 ? "-" : "+"}]{.mono} \${parseFloat(Math.abs(selectedDifference).toFixed(3))}.

In general, the subtrahend is the time of an event such as a rocket🚀launch and the difference is the negative [remaining time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countdown#:~:text=the%20time%20remaining%20before%20an%20event) if the minuend is less than the subtrahend or the positive [elapsed time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_Elapsed_Time) if the minuend is greater than the subtrahend. When the minuend is the current time, the difference acts as a ["T minus (T&minus;) countdown"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countdown#:~:text=a%20sequence%20of%20backward%20counting%20to%20indicate%20the%20time%20remaining) or ["T plus (T+) countup"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countdown#:~:text=After%20a%20launch%2C%20most%20countdown%20clocks%20begin%20to%20show%20Mission%20Elapsed%20Time).

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup18}
$$\begin{cases}\text{difference}<0&{\text{if } \text{minuend} < \text{subtrahend};}\\\text{difference}=0&{\text{if minuend} = \text{subtrahend};}\\\text{difference}>0&{\text{if minuend}>\text{subtrahend.}}\end{cases}$$ {#eq-difference}
:::

Dec uses the term "minuend expansion" to describe the replacement of a minuend with a subtrahend and a difference. We can replace the current [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} with the sum of either zero and a "positive interval [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}" ([pit](#pit){#positiveintervaltod .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="positive interval tod"}), which is the time since the start of the current day (the previous midnight), or 1 day ([d](#d){#day .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day"}) and a "negative interval [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}" ([nit](#nit){#negativeintervaltod .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="negative interval tod"}), which is the negative of the time until the start of the next day (the next midnight), as shown in @eq-todexpansion below. At [Tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} [7]{.color7}, the [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} and [pit](#pit){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="positive interval tod"} are both 0.7 [d](#d){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="days"} and the [nit](#nit){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="negative interval tod"} is -0.3 [d](#d){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="days"}: 0.7 = 0 + 0.7 = 1 + (-0.3).

Likewise, a "positive interval [ada](#ada){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="annual day aggregate"}" ([pia](#pia){#positiveintervalada .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="positive interval ada"}) is the time since the start of the current year and a "negative interval [ada](#ada){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="annual day aggregate"}" ([nia](#nia){#negativeintervalada .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="negative interval ada"}) is the time until the start of the next year. A [pia](#pia){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="positive interval ada"} equals the current [ada](#ada){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="annual day aggregate"}, and a [nia](#nia){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="negative interval ada"} is the [pia](#pia){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="positive interval ada"} minus the [syl](#syl){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar year length"}, just as a "negative integer [doy](#doy){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day of year"}" ([nid](#nid){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="negative integer day of year"}) is a "positive integer [doy](#doy){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="day of year"}" ([pid](#pid){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="positive integer day of year"}) minus the [syl](#syl){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar year length"}. @eq-adaexpansion applies minuend expansion to the [ada](#ada){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="annual day aggregate"}.

::: {.equationgroup #equationgroup19}
$$\text{tod} = 0 + \text{pit} = 1 + \text{nit}$$ {#eq-todexpansion}

$$\text{ada} = 0 + \text{pia} = \text{syl} + \text{nia}$$ {#eq-adaexpansion}
:::

Dec is not the first system to use [decimal time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_time#France). During the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution), decimal time was mandatory for French public records for 197 days ([d](#d){#day .tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="days"}), from [1794]{.color94by100}+[205]{.color205by365} to [1795]{.color95by100}+[037]{.color037by365}. On [1998]{.color98by100}+[247]{.color247by365}, the Swatch corporation announced [Swatch Internet Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swatch_Internet_Time), which divides the day into 1000 ".beats", only has one time zone, and is displayed by Dec as [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"}&minus;&beta;.

Declock measures time in fractional days. A single decimal [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} replaces the three parts of an [hms](#hms){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="hour minute second"} triplet and can be shown at any precision by changing its metric prefix. With one [b9](#b9){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base9"} digit and one [b24](#b24){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base24"} digit after four [b10](#b10){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="base10"} digits, a [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} can match any [hms](#hms){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="hour minute second"} triplet exactly, and a [uto](#uto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="universal time offset"} keeps Dec in step with [ut](#ut){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="Universal Time"} without leap seconds. Dec derives its ten [tzo](#tzo){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time zone offsets"} from longitude and never uses negative offsets, but it lets anyone write any [sto](#sto){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar time offset"} after a [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} without forcing them to change it. With the [eot](#eot){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="equation of time"} and [sda](#sda){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="solar declination angle"}, we can turn a Zone [0]{.color0} [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} into solar time and estimate the [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="times of day"} of sunrise, solar noon, and sunset. Finally, minuend expansion turns any [tod](#tod){.tool data-bs-toggle="tooltip" data-bs-title="time of day"} into a countdown or countup relative to an event, such as midnight, sunset, or the start of the year.
